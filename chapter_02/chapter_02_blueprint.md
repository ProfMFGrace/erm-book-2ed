# Blueprint: How to Write Chapter 2 from Scratch

**Chapter title:** Enterprise Risk Management — Evolution, Frameworks, and Institutional Context  
**Audience:** Graduate or advanced undergraduate students with background in corporate finance (Chapter 1).  
**Pedagogical goal:** Bridge theory (why risk management matters) to practice (how organizations actually do it).

---

## What This Chapter Needs to Do

Chapter 2 establishes the theoretical case for risk management. Chapter 2 has a different job: it answers the organizational and practical question — how do firms actually structure risk management, and against what standards are they evaluated? Students leaving Chapter 2 should be able to describe what an ERM program looks like, explain why it took the form it did, and navigate the frameworks and institutions that practitioners encounter in the field.

There are three things the chapter must accomplish:
1. **Historical context** — ERM has a specific intellectual and regulatory history. Students need to understand it because current practices are direct responses to specific failures. Without that context, ERM frameworks look arbitrary.
2. **Framework fluency** — COSO and ISO 31000 are the lingua franca of ERM. Practitioners use this vocabulary constantly. Students need to know what the frameworks say without being overwhelmed by the full structure of either one.
3. **Institutional awareness** — Who demands ERM, and why? The answer is different in banking versus insurance versus non-financial corporations. Understanding the institutional pressures helps students predict how ERM will actually manifest in different organizations.

---

## Recommended Chapter Architecture

### A. Opening Story (500–800 words)
**Purpose:** Make the problem concrete and memorable before introducing any framework.

A good opening story for this chapter should illustrate the central failure mode that ERM is designed to solve: siloed risk management that leaves no one responsible for the enterprise-level view.

Strong candidate topics:
- **GM ignition switch** (Valukas, 2014): Slow-moving defect. Information existed across multiple functions. No one connected it. 100+ deaths, $3+ billion in consequences. Well-documented. Clear villain is the structure, not any individual.
- **2008 financial crisis (mortgage/CDO)**: Illustrates correlated risk and model failure at scale. Harder to personalize.
- **Boeing 737 MAX**: Regulatory capture and organizational silos. Well-documented but may age.
- **Space Shuttle Challenger**: Classic organizational communication failure. Somewhat dated but still works.

The GM story is recommended because it is clean, well-sourced (Valukas Report is authoritative), and directly illustrates exactly what ERM is designed to prevent: the question "Is this an enterprise-level problem?" belonging to no one.

**Writing guidance:** Narrative prose, not bulleted summary. Do not explain what ERM is in the opening; let the reader feel the problem first. Return to the story at least once later in the chapter (e.g., when discussing governance structures or the importance of risk ownership).

---

### B. Learning Objectives (6–8 items)
**Purpose:** Set expectations and guide student study.

Write these as concrete skills, not vague awareness:
- "Trace the history..." rather than "Understand the evolution..."
- Use verbs: trace, explain, describe, compare, identify, outline, apply.

Keep to 6–8. More than 8 signals that the chapter is trying to do too much.

---

### C. Chapter Overview (300–400 words)
**Purpose:** Give students a roadmap. Should be brief, specific, and written last.

Tell the student exactly what the chapter covers and why it matters relative to what came before and what comes next. Avoid generic statements about ERM being important. Be specific about the historical arc, the two frameworks, and the institutional distinction between financial and non-financial sectors.

---

### D. Section 1: History (1,000–1,500 words)

**Three movements to cover:**

**1.1 The siloed origins (pre-1990)**
- Insurance purchasing as the dominant paradigm
- Risk manager as insurance buyer, not strategic function
- Emergence of captives and self-insurance in 1970s–80s — cost optimization, not strategic integration

**1.2 Failures that drove change (1990–2003)**

Cover at least three: one financial derivatives disaster (Barings 1995 or Orange County 1994), the Enron/WorldCom governance collapse, and the common thread among them — information existed, authority to act was fragmented.

Do not try to cover every scandal. Pick the clearest examples and be specific about amounts and mechanisms. Vague references to "high-profile failures" are useless pedagogically.

**1.3 Sarbanes-Oxley (2002) as inflection point**
- Section 302 (executive certification) and Section 404 (internal control assessment) are the two provisions that matter for risk management
- SOX did not mandate ERM; it created infrastructure and accountability norms that made ERM more natural and more expected
- The broader shift from disclosure-based to rules-based regulatory philosophy has implications beyond financial reporting

**Writing guidance:** This section should read like engaged history, not a timeline. Explain the mechanisms — why did Barings collapse, not just that it collapsed. The word "siloed" should appear early and be defined by example before it is named as a concept.

---

### E. Section 2: Frameworks (1,500–2,000 words)

**2.1 COSO**
- Brief institutional history: five accounting/audit associations, *Internal Control — Integrated Framework* (1992), *ERM — Integrated Framework* (2004), 2017 update
- The 2017 update's key contribution: ERM as value creation, not just loss prevention; risk appetite as a strategic positive choice
- Present the five components in a **box** (see below) — this breaks up the text and signals to readers that this is reference material to return to, not narrative to absorb linearly
- After the box: discuss in prose what the framework gets right, what it leaves out, and why the 2017 update changed the framing

**Box design guidance:**
- Title: "Box 2.1 — The COSO ERM Framework: Five Components and Twenty Principles"
- Format: Name + one-paragraph description for each of the five components
- End with a note on the 20 principles as an interconnected system, not a checklist
- Keep each component description to 3–5 sentences
- Do not reproduce the 20 individual principle names; that level of detail belongs in an appendix or the original document

**2.2 ISO 31000**
- International standard (2009, updated 2018); more generic and flexible than COSO
- Definition of risk: "effect of uncertainty on objectives" — explain what this captures that narrower definitions miss
- Three-layer structure: principles, framework, process
- The process model (scope/context/criteria → identification → analysis → evaluation → treatment → monitoring) is ISO's most useful contribution for practitioners — more explicit about methodology than COSO
- Behavioral/cultural factors are explicit in the ISO principles; worth highlighting because organizations often miss this

**2.3 Comparison**
- COSO: governance-oriented, U.S.-focused, 20 explicit principles, prescriptive
- ISO: process-oriented, international, flexible, more method-explicit
- Practical reality: complement each other, often used together
- Important caveat: neither framework provides rigorous empirical evidence that organizations following their prescriptions outperform those that do not. Note this explicitly.

---

### F. Section 3: Institutional Context (800–1,200 words)

This section answers: who requires or encourages ERM, and how?

**Financial services (regulatory mandate):**
- Basel Committee: Basel I → II → III, and what changed at each step
- U.S. banking regulators (Fed, OCC, FDIC) and Dodd-Frank stress testing
- NAIC and ORSA for insurance
- Solvency II for European insurers
- Common thread: capital requirements calibrated to risk, supervisory review, forward-looking stress tests

**Non-financial corporations (voluntary but not casual):**
- RIMS: professional home for corporate risk managers; Risk Maturity Model
- IIA: internal audit as third line; Three Lines Model
- Credit rating agencies: ERM as a factor in ratings methodology
- Proxy advisors and institutional investors: board risk oversight as governance expectation
- Common thread: converging pressures from multiple directions without a single legal mandate

**Writing guidance:** Keep this section analytically organized rather than encyclopedic. The interesting observation is the institutional difference between financial and non-financial sectors — use that contrast to frame the section, not just list organizations.

---

### G. Section 4 (Optional): Implementing ERM in Practice (600–900 words)

This section bridges from frameworks to action. Recommended content:
- Phased implementation (Phase 1 governance basics → Phase 2 risk identification → Phase 3 ownership and responses → Phase 4 integration into business processes → Phase 5 quantitative sophistication)
- Structural options: centralized CRO, distributed, hybrid — with trade-offs
- Risk culture as the hardest problem: leadership behavior, incentive systems
- Measurement: process metrics, outcome metrics, maturity assessments — and the limits of each

If the chapter is running long, this section can be abbreviated or moved to an end-of-chapter supplement.

---

### H. Summary and Key Takeaways

- Chapter summary: 2–3 short paragraphs covering the historical arc, the frameworks, and the institutional context. Should read like the chapter's actual argument, not a table of contents in sentence form.
- Key takeaways: 5–7 bullets. Direct, specific, no throat-clearing.

## What to Avoid

**Do not reproduce the COSO 20 principles in sequential list form.** The old chapter 2 did this at length. The result was accurate but unreadable — it looked like a manual, not a chapter. The framework box should summarize the components; deeper COSO detail belongs in an appendix if it belongs anywhere.

**Do not let the history section become a timeline.** Students need to understand the mechanisms, not just the dates. Why did Enron collapse? What exactly did the Barings trader do? What made Sarbanes-Oxley a meaningful change rather than symbolic legislation?

**Do not treat ISO and COSO as equal alternatives.** In U.S. corporate practice, COSO is primary; ISO is supplementary. Acknowledge ISO's broader international applicability without overstating its domestic use.

**Do not end with "In conclusion, this chapter has shown..." or equivalent.** The summary section should stand as the actual argument, not an announcement that the argument happened.

## Notes on Tone and Style

This chapter is inherently reference-heavy, but it should not read like a framework manual. The history section should have narrative momentum. The framework section should explain *why* the frameworks are structured as they are, not just *what* they contain. The institutional section should use the financial/non-financial contrast as an organizing argument.

The GM story should do real work. It should not disappear after the opening; return to it at least once when discussing governance structures or the importance of assigned ownership.

Where the evidence is thin — particularly on whether ERM actually improves performance — say so plainly. The Hoyt & Liebenberg (2011) paper provides some evidence of a positive relationship between ERM adoption and Tobin's Q, but the identification is challenging and the results are not definitive. Students benefit from seeing that academic uncertainty noted honestly.
