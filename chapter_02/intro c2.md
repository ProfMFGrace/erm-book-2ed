It began with a small spring.

Inside the ignition switch of a Chevrolet Cobalt, a tiny coiled spring was supposed to hold the key firmly in the "run" position while the car was in motion. The engineers who designed it knew, before the Cobalt ever reached a showroom, that the spring was too weak. Turn the key with a heavy keychain, nudge it with your knee, hit a rough patch of road at the wrong moment — and the switch could slip. The engine would cut out. The power steering would go. And, most fatally, the airbags would not deploy.

General Motors first documented the problem in 2001. The Cobalt went on sale in 2004. The recall did not come until February 2014.

In the thirteen years between those two dates, something remarkable happened — or rather, failed to happen. Engineers analyzed stalling complaints and noted the switch-torque problem in technical reports. Field service bulletins went out to dealers. Customers called hotlines to describe their cars dying on the highway. Lawyers at GM received claims and handled wrongful death litigation involving crashes where airbags had inexplicably failed to deploy. Safety committees reviewed incident data and submitted reports to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. Internal review after internal review touched the edges of the same problem.

And yet no one connected the dots.

The independent investigation that GM commissioned in the aftermath — conducted by attorney Anton Valukas and released in 2014 — described what it found inside the company in terms that should chill any student of risk management. It was not a story of a single villain who knew everything and stayed silent. It was something more troubling: a large organization in which many competent people were each managing a piece of a serious risk, no one was responsible for managing the whole of it, and the institution drifted for more than a decade while people died.

Engineers owned the technical problem. Lawyers owned the claims. Safety personnel owned the regulatory relationship. Product managers owned the brand. Each group worked within its lane, incurring real costs — conducting analyses, settling cases, filing reports, attending meetings — and each group saw only the slice of the problem that landed on its desk. The critical question — Is this a single enterprise-level safety risk that requires a decision at the top of this company? — belonged to no one. And so it went unasked.

This is a siloed approach to risk management, and the GM ignition switch crisis is one of the most costly illustrations of it in modern corporate history. By the time GM finally acted, the company faced more than 100 deaths linked to the defect, a $900 million settlement with the U.S. Department of Justice, a $2.5 billion victim compensation fund, and congressional hearings that put its leadership on national television to explain how a company with thousands of engineers, lawyers, and safety professionals had failed to fix a broken spring for thirteen years.

The answer, as the Valukas Report made clear, was not a shortage of smart people or a shortage of activity. It was a shortage of integration. The information existed. The expertise existed. What did not exist was a structure that forced the scattered pieces into a single picture, assigned one person or one process the authority to ask the enterprise-level question, and created accountability for the answer.

That structure has a name. It is called enterprise risk management.
