It started with a red envelope.

In the early 2000s, millions of Americans got used to seeing slim red mailers from a company called Netflix in their mailboxes. Customers went online, picked a few DVDs, and within days the discs arrived. When they were done, they slipped the discs back into the same envelopes and sent them through the U.S. mail. No late fees. No wandering the aisles of a video store on Friday night. For a while, it looked like the future.

For Netflix, the DVD‑by‑mail business was not a side project. It was the business. By the mid‑2000s, the company had millions of subscribers, growing revenues, and a clear foil in Blockbuster, whose brick‑and‑mortar rental stores suddenly seemed slow and expensive. Netflix’s logistics system for buying, sorting, and shipping discs was a sophisticated operation. Wall Street liked the story. The numbers were good. The red envelope had become a small cultural icon.

Inside the company, however, some people were already worrying about what came next.

As early as the mid‑2000s, Netflix executives and engineers were tracking a trend that had nothing to do with postal routes: the steady spread of high‑speed internet connections into American homes and the rapid improvement of video compression technology. In 2000, most customers could not watch a movie over the internet without constant buffering. A few years later, broadband penetration was rising, home networks were more common, and the idea of watching full‑length video online no longer sounded absurd. If movies and television could be delivered instantly, over the internet, what would that do to a business built around putting plastic discs into the mail? 

The risk was strategic and operational, not just technical. If Netflix clung to DVDs for too long, a rival—perhaps a cable company with on‑demand infrastructure, perhaps a technology firm with deep pockets—could leapfrog it with a better streaming service. If Netflix moved too quickly, investing heavily in streaming before customers and content owners were ready, it could undermine the DVD cash cow that was funding its growth. The company faced a classic problem of disruption: how do you intentionally erode the business model that currently pays your bills, in order to build the one that might keep you alive? 

Netflix did something that many firms struggle to do. It named this as a concrete, enterprise‑level risk. In board discussions, strategic planning, and eventually in the Risk Factors section of its Form 10‑K, management identified a cluster of related uncertainties: that broadband might not spread as fast as expected; that studios might restrict or overprice digital rights; that competitors with “greater financial, marketing, and technical resources” could outcompete Netflix in streaming; and that the shift from physical to digital distribution could alienate customers. These were not explanations written after the fact. They were early, forward‑looking statements of risk, made while the DVD business still looked strong. 
Acting on that risk identification meant spending money before the payoff was certain. Netflix began hiring engineers to build a streaming platform, negotiating licenses for online content, and experimenting with an early “Watch Instantly” feature that let subscribers view some titles on their computers instead of waiting for a disc. All of this investment came while the red envelopes were still central to the company’s revenue. In its public filings, Netflix warned investors that the DVD business could decline faster than expected and that its future depended on successfully managing the transition to streaming. In effect, it told shareholders: the engine that powers our current earnings is at risk, and we are deliberately trying to replace it. 

The transition was messy. In 2011, Netflix tried to split its DVD and streaming services into separate brands, a move it argued was necessary to prepare for a streaming‑dominated future. Customers rebelled at the price changes and confusion. The company backtracked, and its stock price plunged. But the underlying risk assessment—the belief that physical discs were a shrinking technology and that streaming would define the market—did not change. Netflix continued to invest in streaming technology, expand into new countries, and eventually produce its own original programming. 

A decade after those first experiments, the red envelopes were disappearing. Streaming had become the default way many people watched movies and television. Blockbuster’s stores were gone. Cable and satellite providers were wrestling with “cord‑cutting.” Netflix, the former mail‑order DVD company, had become a global streaming platform and media producer, with a brand associated not with the mailbox, but with binge‑watching. The DVD business, once central, was eventually shut down entirely. 

From an enterprise risk management perspective, the story is not just about innovation. It is about **identification**. Netflix recognized, early and explicitly, that its own successful business model contained a built‑in obsolescence risk. It treated that recognition as a strategic risk, wrote it down in its risk disclosures, and used it to justify reallocating capital and attention before the old model had obviously failed. In doing so, it navigated a transition that has been fatal for many incumbents in other industries. The Netflix case illustrates that some of the most important risks do not arrive as sudden crises. They arrive as slow‑moving shifts in technology and behavior that turn yesterday’s strengths into tomorrow’s vulnerabilities—and that the hardest part of managing them is simply seeing them in time.
